          When your idea gets serious

          A dated notebook is a good record of your work, and it helps show the idea is yours. It does not protect the idea. In the U.S., when two people invent the same thing, the first inventor to file with the Patent Office usually wins, not the first to write it down. So once you think your invention might be worth patenting or selling:

          • Keep a second, dated copy. Download the text version and a backup after each work session, and keep them with a parent. Many inventors also keep a bound paper notebook with numbered pages, written in pen.
          • Don't show it publicly yet. Posting, presenting, or selling it starts a clock on patents (see the Playbook question on patents). Talk to a parent and a mentor first.
          • Be careful what you send. The mentor email shares a short summary only. Leave out how it works until you've talked to your mentor, and don't paste the details into a public AI tool.
          • Get a real adviser. Mentors at Stage 4 include alumni attorneys and founders who can tell you whether to file, and what it costs.

          The two email buttons open your email app with your project summary filled in. Nothing is sent until you, or the parent you copy, press send. In the Invention Scouts plan this record is called a Supervised Invention Experience: the project that mentors review, judges score, and the ladder counts.

          The Playbook

          How to do the hard parts

          Thirteen questions every young inventor runs into, in the order you will probably hit them. Each answer is a starting point drawn from people who do this for a living: the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office, product designers, engineers, and founders. Follow the links to go deeper.

          Start here

          Source: Paul Graham, How to Get Startup Ideas, and the Invention Scouts approach

          You don't need an idea to start. You need a problem, and the best ones are noticed, not dreamed up. Inventors who last start with something that bugs real people, often themselves.

          1. Keep a problem list for two weeks. Every time something annoys you, wastes your time, or makes you say "why is this so hard?", write it down. At camp, at practice, in the kitchen, in the troop trailer. Aim for twenty.
          2. Notice your own problems first. You can test every version on the person who cares most: you. Louis Braille, Chester Greenwood, and Ann Makosinski all started this way.
          3. Look for problems lots of people have, often. A problem that bugs one person once a year is small. One that bugs your whole patrol every campout is worth a notebook.
          4. Problems that hurt people beat problems that annoy them. Margaret Knight was twelve when she saw a mill worker injured and designed a safety stop. Safety, health, and accessibility problems matter.
          5. Write it in one sentence. "[Who] has trouble [doing what] because [why]." If you can't write that sentence, keep looking. If you can, you're in Stage 1.

          Run the Problem Hunt challenge with your patrol. Twenty Scouts' problems in forty-five minutes beats a month of thinking alone.

          Source: USPTO patent search guidance and Google Patents

          Probably, at least partly. That's good news: it proves the problem is real, and you get to learn from how other people tried to solve it. What you're looking for is what is still missing.

          1. Search stores first. Type the problem, not your solution, into two or three online stores: "pull tent stakes frozen ground." If there's a product, read the one-star reviews. That's a list of what it gets wrong.
          2. Then search patents. Google Patents searches millions of patents in plain words. Try five different phrasings. Old, expired patents count too.
          3. Read the drawings, not the legal text. Patent drawings show you exactly how someone else solved it. That's free engineering advice.
          4. List what's different about yours. Cheaper? Lighter? Easier for a younger Scout? Works in the cold? Write it in your notebook. That list becomes your pitch.
          5. Found an exact match? Don't quit. Change the user, the setting, or the price. Or pick the next problem on your list. Every search makes you better at the next one.

          Anything already public before your invention is called prior art. You'll come back to this search in Stage 4 if you think about a patent.

          Source: the lab-notebook practice of engineers and inventors, and the Inventing merit badge

          A notebook is a record of what you did and why you did it, written as you go. It is how you remember what you tried, how a mentor helps you, and how judges see your thinking. Engineers keep them for the same reasons.

          1. Date every entry. Even one line counts. "Nov 9. Built v1. Hurts your hand."
          2. Write what you did and what you learned. The second part is what makes it an inventor's notebook, not a diary. What surprised you? What will you change?
          3. Number your versions. v1, v2, v3. Judges love to see how far version 3 is from version 1.
          4. Record people, not just parts. Who did you talk to, what did they do with it, what did they say? Use first names or "Scout, age 12."
          5. Never erase. Cross out. A wrong turn is part of the story. If you use paper, keep sketches, photos, and test results in it too.

          The notebook in this guidebook saves on your device and prints. Some Scouts keep a paper one too and photograph the sketches. Either way, keep it going after the badge is signed.

          Build it

          Source: product-design practice (IDEO, Stanford d.school): build to think

          Designers say "build to think." You learn more from a bad model you can hold than from a perfect drawing. The trick is to start cheap and only spend time and money once you know what you're making.

          1. Sketch three different ideas. Not three versions of one idea: three different ways to solve the problem. Then pick.
          2. Build ugly, in an hour. Cardboard, tape, clay, paper clips, foam. If it takes longer than an hour, you're building too much too soon.
          3. Separate "looks like" from "works like." One model to check the size and shape, another (maybe a mess of wires on a table) to prove the mechanism. Combine them later.
          4. Then use the real tools. Design it in Tinkercad or Onshape and print it, cut it on a laser, or build it in the wood or metal shop. Show your counselor your plans first.
          5. Test one thing at a time. Change the handle or the material, not both. Otherwise you won't know which change worked.

          Most people use a 3D printer to print a model someone else designed and posted online. Print your own design instead. That's the difference between using a printer and inventing.

          Start in Tinkercad →

          Source: Scouting America's Guide to Safe Scouting and makerspace practice

          Laser engravers, saws, soldering irons, welders, and even 3D printers can hurt you. Every good maker follows the same rules, every time, including the adults.

          1. Get the briefing first. Never use a tool until a trained adult has shown you how and watched you use it. Ask every time a tool is new to you.
          2. Dress for the shop. Safety glasses on. Long hair tied back. No loose sleeves, hoodies strings, or dangling jewelry near anything that spins.
          3. Never alone. Use the buddy system and work where a trained adult can see you. Welding and metalwork are for older Scouts with trained supervision.
          4. Know the tool's specific danger. Lasers can start fires: never leave one running. Soldering irons stay hot after they're off. Some plastics give off fumes when cut or melted: ask before you cut anything new.
          5. Get your counselor's OK before the real build. For Inventing, share your design and building plans with your counselor and get approval before you build the prototype.

          If something feels unsafe, stop and ask. Nobody who knows what they're doing will think less of you. They stop and ask too.

          Source: James Dyson on prototyping, and the Invention Scouts finding principle

          Celebrate a little. A prototype that breaks just told you something no amount of thinking could. James Dyson has said he built 5,127 prototypes of his bagless vacuum over about five years before one worked well enough.

          1. Write down exactly how it failed. Where did it crack? When? Under what load? That's a notebook entry, and it's the most useful kind.
          2. Find the cause, not the symptom. The handle cracked. Why? Too thin? Wrong material? Printed in the weak direction? Ask "why" three times.
          3. Change one thing, then test again. That's an iteration. Number it.
          4. Ask someone who's seen it before. Your Invention Ranger, a counselor, or a mentor has probably broken the same thing. Bring the broken part.
          5. Know when to switch. If five versions all fail the same way, the idea might need to change, not the part. Go back to your sketches. That's still progress.

          The program's founder put it simply: the Scout who keeps a project alive across two summers is the inventor. Breaking things is how you get there.

          Prove it

          Source: Rob Fitzpatrick, The Mom Test

          People are nice. If you ask "would you use this?", most will say yes to be kind. The Mom Test is a way to ask questions that even your mom can't lie to you about.

          1. Ask about the past, not the future. "When did that last happen to you? What did you do?" beats "Would you buy this?" every time.
          2. Don't describe your idea first. The moment you pitch, people start being polite. Learn about their problem, then show it.
          3. Watch, don't explain. Hand someone your prototype and say nothing. Where they get stuck is your next version.
          4. Count what they do, not what they say. Did they ask to borrow it again? Ask where to get one? That's real. "Cool!" isn't.
          5. Talk to people with a buddy, near adults you know. Interview Scouts at meetings and campouts, family, and neighbors with a parent. Don't message strangers online.

          Five honest conversations will teach you more than fifty compliments. Run the Five Interviews challenge to practice.

          Read about The Mom Test →

          Source: the Entrepreneurship merit badge business plan, shortened, and Trulaske CEI mentors

          A business case answers one question: could this work outside your garage? You don't need a business to write one. One page, seven headings. The Entrepreneurship merit badge asks for the longer version.

          1. The problem. Your one sentence, plus the proof: "5 of 6 Scouts I asked bent a stake this year."
          2. Who it's for. The user, and the customer if it's someone else (the troop committee, a parent).
          3. What it is. Your invention in two sentences, and what's different from what exists.
          4. What it costs to make one. Parts, packaging, and your time. The Workbench's Price it tab does the math.
          5. What you'd charge, and why. What do similar things cost? What did people say they paid last time?
          6. How people would find out. A booth at camp, the troop newsletter, the council trading post, a family member's shop.
          7. What's next, and what you need. Version 3? Ten units for a test? A mentor? A competition?

          This is the half of inventing most camps don't teach, and the half Trulaske mentors are best at. Bring your draft to a mentor session.

          Source: pitch practice from startup competitions, adapted for Scout invention competitions

          A pitch is a short story with five beats. Ninety seconds is enough if every sentence earns its place. Judges remember the problem and the proof, not the adjectives.

          1. The problem (15 seconds). Make them feel it. "Raise your hand if you've ever bent a tent stake." Start with a person, not a product.
          2. Who has it (10 seconds). How many, how often. Use the number from your interviews.
          3. Your fix (25 seconds). Show it. Hold it up. Demonstrate it if you can. One sentence on what's different.
          4. Proof (25 seconds). What happened when real people used it. Version 1 versus version 3. Your notebook is your evidence.
          5. What's next (15 seconds). What it costs, what you'd charge, and the one thing you need: a mentor, a batch of 50, a test at camp.

          Practice out loud, five times, with a timer. Then once in front of your patrol. Your voice will shake less the fifth time than the first. That's the whole secret.

          No, and you shouldn't. The founder of Invention Scouts points out that inventors are rare, but every inventor needs a team of engineers, artists, business people, marketers, and salespeople. Not every Scout will be the inventor. Every Scout can be on the team.

          1. Use the patrol method. Pick a problem together, then split the roles: a builder, a tester, a designer for the look and the poster, and a pitcher.
          2. Find your role. Maybe you're the one who sees how to make it, or the one who can explain it to anyone, or the one who keeps the notebook. All of these are inventor jobs.
          3. Agree who does what, in writing. One line each in the notebook. It prevents the "I did all the work" argument later.
          4. Credit everyone. If it gets entered, sold, or written up, every teammate's name goes on it.
          5. Decide together what happens if it takes off. If the team might sell it or file a patent, talk to your parents and a mentor early. Fairness is easier before there's money.

          The summer-camp Startup program is built around small teams for exactly this reason. Its goal isn't only inventors; it's Scouts who know how to work as a team and lead.

          Launch it · with a parent and a mentor

          Source: U.S. Patent and Trademark Office, Patent Basics and scam-prevention guidance

          Maybe, but probably not yet. A patent is expensive, takes years, and only matters if someone would want to copy your invention and sell it. Most young inventors should first prove people want it. You can be named as an inventor at any age, but because there are forms, fees, and contracts, a parent or guardian is part of every step.

          1. Know what a patent does. It lets you stop others from making, using, or selling your invention for a limited time (a utility patent usually lasts 20 years from filing), in exchange for publishing how it works.
          2. Be careful about showing it publicly. Posting a video, selling it, or presenting it in public is a public disclosure. In the U.S. you have one year after your own disclosure to file; many other countries give you no time at all. Talk to a parent and a mentor before you post it.
          3. Know the cheaper first step. A provisional application holds your filing date for 12 months while you test whether the invention is worth more. It never becomes a patent on its own. The USPTO publishes its fees and has discounts for small inventors.
          4. Watch for scams. Companies that advertise "We'll patent and sell your idea!" often charge thousands up front and deliver little. The USPTO warns about them and publishes complaints. Never pay one; tell an adult.
          5. Get real help. Mentors include alumni attorneys and founders who can tell you whether a patent makes sense. The Invention Scouts patent stage points toward the National Academy of Inventors' certificate training.

          The customer conversations from Stage 3 answer the most important patent question: would anyone pay for this? Answer that before you spend a dollar.

          Source: design-for-manufacture practice, and the proposed Invention Scouts launch track

          Making one is inventing. Making fifty is manufacturing, and it's a different puzzle: every minute and every cent gets multiplied by fifty.

          1. Write the bill of materials. Every part, how many, what each costs, and where you buy it. Buying fifty is often cheaper per part than buying one.
          2. Simplify the design. Fewer parts, fewer screws, snap-fits instead of glue. Print in the direction that's strongest and fastest. This is called design for manufacture.
          3. Time yourself making five. The fifth will be faster than the first. Multiply your fifth time by fifty. Is that a weekend or a whole summer?
          4. Make a jig. A simple guide that holds parts in the same place every time makes every unit the same and saves hours.
          5. Make ten before fifty. Sell or give away ten and see what happens. Then decide on fifty. The Workbench's Batch tab shows where you break even.

          A proposed twelve-month launch track would give Scouts with a working prototype an engineering review, a small-batch run, and a place to sell. It's named for Chess2Go, the portable chess set dreamed up at a Missouri campsite.

          Source: the Salesmanship and American Business merit badges, and Trulaske CEI mentors

          There are two ways to get an invention into the world: sell it yourself, or license it to a company that makes and sells it and pays you a small share. Both involve money and agreements, so both happen with a parent or guardian.

          1. Make your first ten sales by hand. A table at a craft fair, a booth at an event, friends of your family. Selling face to face teaches you what people really think, fast.
          2. Keep your invention business separate from Scouting fundraising. Scouting has rules about using its name, logos, and uniform to sell things. Ask your Scoutmaster before you use any of them.
          3. Let a parent handle the money side. Payments, accounts, and anything with a card number. Keep a simple record in your notebook of what you sold and spent.
          4. Know what licensing means. A company makes and sells your invention and pays you a royalty, often a few percent of each sale. Companies usually want proof people buy it, and often a patent or a filing.
          5. Never sign anything alone. Any agreement, any "partner" offering to help, any request for money: show your parent and a mentor first.

          Many inventions never get licensed, and many great inventors never sell anything. The skills you build by trying (talking to people, pricing, pitching) last a lifetime either way.

          A Power Tool

          Using AI like an inventor

          AI chat tools can explain how a hinge works at midnight, quiz you before a pitch, and help you plan your next two weeks. Like any power tool, they have rules, and they're useless if they do the work for you. A merit badge counselor needs to see your thinking, not a chatbot's. A Scout is trustworthy.

          Last reviewed . New: the Artificial Intelligence merit badge.

          Source: AI providers' terms of use, Scouting's Youth Protection rules, and patent basics

          1. Check the age rules. Most popular AI chat tools require you to be at least 13, and many require a parent's permission until you're 18. Read the tool's terms with a parent, or use the account your school gives you.
          2. Keep personal details out. No last name, address, school, phone number, or photos of yourself. Assume anything you type may be stored.
          3. Keep secret inventions secret. If you might want to patent or sell your invention, talk to a mentor before pasting its details into a public AI tool.
          4. Check everything it says. AI can sound sure and be completely wrong, including about safety, patents, and badge requirements. Check with a real source or a real adult.
          5. Do your own work. Use it to learn and to practice, never to write your badge requirements, your interviews, or your notebook. If it's not your thinking, it's not your invention.

          Source: Trulaske CEI mentors

          Good jobs: explaining how a mechanism or a circuit works; asking you questions that help you notice problems; suggesting search phrases for Google Patents; reviewing your design by asking what could break; quizzing you on your pitch; checking your cost math step by step; making a two-week plan.

          Bad jobs: inventing the idea for you; writing answers to badge requirements; making up what users "would say"; deciding whether something is safe to build; telling you whether to file a patent; anything with money or contracts.

          A good test: if you couldn't explain the answer to your counselor without the AI open, you haven't learned it yet.

          Safety First

          Safe, every time

          Know the rules the adults follow, so you know what normal looks like. If something ever feels wrong, you can always say no, leave, and tell someone.

          Rules every mentor follows

          • Every mentor completes Scouting America Youth Protection Training and registers with the council before working with Scouts.
          • Two-deep leadership: at least two registered adults at every activity.
          • No adult is ever alone with you, in person, online, by text, or by video.
          • A parent or another leader is copied on every email or message to you.
          • Mentors meet teams in public program spaces, like the Innovation Space, never at their home or yours.

          If something feels wrong

          • You can say no to anything that makes you uncomfortable, and leave.
          • Tell your parent, your Scoutmaster, or another adult you trust, right away.
          • Scouting America's Scouts First Helpline, 24 hours, call or text: 1-844-SCOUTS1 (1-844-726-8871).
          • If anyone is in danger, call 911.

          Your privacy on this page

          • This page sends nothing anywhere. Your path, notebook, ladder, and calculator numbers are saved only in this browser.
          • No tracking or analytics run on this page.
          • The email buttons open your own email app with a draft. Nothing is sent until you, or the parent you copy, press send.
          • Use your first name only. On a shared computer, download a backup and clear the notebook when you're done.

          Shop, money, and online

          • Never use a new tool without a trained adult's briefing, safety glasses, and a buddy.
          • Never pay, or let anyone ask your family to pay, a company to "patent and market" your idea.
          • Never sign anything, give card numbers, or agree to a "partnership" without a parent.
          • Don't message strangers about your invention online. Interviews happen with people you and your parents know.

          For Parents, Leaders & Mentors

          For the adults

          The guidebook is written for Scouts, but it works best with an adult nearby. Here is how it fits the Invention Scouts program and what each of you can do.

          How the guidebook lines up with the Invention Scouts four-stage model

          Invention Scouts stageGuidebook stagesWhat happens
          1 · Find and grow0 Curious, 1 Find it, 2 Build itMerit badges, unit challenges, the summer-camp Startup program, Invention Rangers in units
          2 · Competitions and higher-level growth3 Prove itThe competition ladder from unit to national, user testing, the business case, invention-focused Eagle projects
          3 · Patents4 Launch itPrior-art search, plain-language IP, professional inventors and attorneys, the NAI certificate pathway
          4 · Commercialization and licensing4 Launch itMaking in quantity, first sales, licensing, with mentors throughout

          Parents and guardians

          • Ask your Scout to show you their notebook. It's the best window into what they're learning.
          • Everything on this page stays on your device. Nothing collects your Scout's information.
          • Mentor requests and "I built something" emails are drafts that ask you to be copied. Mentors reply to you and your Scout together.
          • Anything with money, sales, accounts, patents, or contracts needs you. The guidebook says so at every step.

          Scoutmasters and Invention Rangers

          • Run a patrol challenge at a meeting; each ends with a prompt to start a notebook.
          • Review notebooks once a month. Ask what changed between versions and why.
          • Sign ladder rungs from the printed sheet (pilot; the council sets final requirements).
          • Nominate a Scout who keeps building after the badge is signed. That Scout is the inventor.

          Mentors

          • Complete Youth Protection Training, register with the council, and take the two-hour CEI mentor module before any contact.
          • Coach, don't do. Ask questions; let the Scout make the decisions and the notebook entries.
          • Meet teams in program spaces with two adults present. Copy a parent on every message.
          • Refer upward when a Scout needs it: student mentor to faculty to alumni founder or attorney.

          Other councils and schools

          • The guidebook is council-agnostic: the content lives in plain data files and can be adapted in an afternoon.
          • Any council adding Invention Scouts can adopt it, and any business school can plug in as the mentor partner.
          • See ADOPTING.md in the source repository.

          Got a problem that bugs you? Invent the fix.

          You don't need a lab, a business, or a big idea. Four ways to start this week, for Scouts aged 12 to 18:

          1. Run a Problem Hunt with your patrol: twenty real problems from the last campout in 45 minutes.
          2. Open an Inventor's Notebook in the guidebook. One dated entry: the problem and who has it.
          3. Earn Inventing. Ask your Scoutmaster for a counselor, or sign up at a council merit badge session.
          4. Build at the Innovation Space in Columbia, at the Invention Campus at the Lake, or when an Invention Lab trailer visits.
